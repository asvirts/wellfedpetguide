Dog food brands to avoid are easier to describe as a pattern than as a hall of shame. A list of villains goes stale the week a formula changes, and a list built from invented taste tests is worse. The brands worth leaving on the shelf are the ones that fail a few public checks any owner can do with the bag and a phone.

"Best dog food brands" is the search next door, and it is mostly advertising. This page takes the more useful half of that search: what to buy instead, after you know what to walk past. If the dog is sick today, start with [what to feed a dog with diarrhea](/dog-food-for-diarrhea/) or [a dog that is not eating](/dog-not-eating/) before you rebuild the whole pantry.

## The checks that matter more than the brand name

AAFCO, the Association of American Feed Control Officials, publishes the model rules that state feed programs use. It does not grade taste, and it does not run a secret lab for this website. What it does publish, on the [selecting a pet food](https://www.aafco.org/consumers/understanding-pet-food/selecting-the-right-pet-food/) and [reading labels](https://www.aafco.org/consumers/understanding-pet-food/reading-labels/) pages, is how a label is supposed to tell you the food is complete.

Read these on the bag you are holding:

- A nutritional adequacy statement that names the species and the life stage. "Complete and balanced" for adult maintenance is not the same claim as a food for growth, and an intermittent snack does not have to be complete.
- Whether that statement says the food was formulated to meet nutrient profiles or that it was tested in feeding trials. Profiles are the common route. Feeding trials are a higher bar for some questions, and they are not magic.
- A calorie statement, so you can feed the dog in front of you rather than the dog on the bag photo.
- A lot code and a way to contact the manufacturer. If a recall hits, those are the only parts of the design that matter.

The FDA's page on [complete and balanced pet food](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/complete-and-balanced-pet-food) says the same thing in regulator language. A food can be "natural," "human grade," or photographed on a walnut table and still fail to be a complete diet. Human grade is a handling claim. It is not a health outcome.

The WSAVA nutrition toolkit asks the questions a clinic should be able to get answered: who formulates the food, what expertise they have, and whether the company can show nutrient analysis and research rather than a slogan. You can ask those questions yourself. A company that will not answer them is a company to stop buying from, even if the bag is pretty.

## Patterns that belong in the avoid pile

### No adequacy statement, or the wrong life stage

A food marketed as everyday dinner, with no statement that it is complete for your dog's life stage, does not belong in the bowl as the only diet. Treats and toppers are allowed to be incomplete. Dinner is not. Puppy foods and adult foods are not swaps. A large-breed puppy has different growth needs than a senior terrier, which is why a later guide will cover large-breed puppy food on its own.

### The story is doing the work of the nutrient panel

"No by-products," "ancestral," and "the ingredients you would eat" are marketing. By-products have a regulatory definition. Some are poor ingredients and some are nutritious organs. You cannot tell which from the absence of the word. Ingredient lists are ordered by weight before cooking, so a fresh meat can outrank a dry meal and still contribute less protein to the finished kibble. Tufts has spent years telling owners to stop judging a food by the romance of the ingredient list. Their hospital nutrition service is here: [Cummings School nutrition](https://vet.tufts.edu/foster-hospital-small-animals/specialty-services/nutrition).

### Grain-free as a health claim

Grain-free dog food is a huge search because owners were told grains are fillers. Grains are ingredients. Some dogs do better on a specific diet for a diagnosed reason. Most do not need a grain-free food to be healthy.

The FDA opened an investigation into a potential link between certain diets and canine dilated cardiomyopathy. Many of the reported diets were grain-free and high in peas, lentils, or potatoes. The investigation is not a ban, and it is not proof that every grain-free bag causes heart disease. It is also not something to shrug off because a brand says "taurine added." Read the [FDA investigation page](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/outbreaks-and-advisories/fda-investigation-potential-link-between-certain-diets-and-canine-dilated-cardiomyopathy) before you treat grain-free as the premium default. A dog with a heart murmur, a breed with a known DCM risk, or a dog already eating one of those diets should be discussed with a veterinarian, not with a product roundup.

### Recalls, read correctly

The FDA publishes [recalls and withdrawals](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/safety-health/recalls-withdrawals). A recall means a specific product, lot, and hazard. It can be Salmonella, aflatoxin, vitamin D, or a labeling error. Check the lot if you own that food. Do not promote a years-old recall into a permanent character judgment, and do not ignore a current one because you like the company. Brands that make it hard to find the lot code are brands that fail the practical test.

### Raw and homemade as the only dinner, without a recipe

AVMA policy on [raw or undercooked animal-source proteins](https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/avma-policies/raw-or-undercooked-animal-source-protein-cat-and-dog-diets) is concerned with pathogens for pets and for the people who live with them. A raw diet can be complete if it is formulated that way. A pile of meat and vegetable scraps usually is not. If you want that topic, it deserves its own guide and a conversation with a veterinary nutritionist. It does not belong as a casual replacement for a recalled kibble on a Saturday night.

## What to buy instead

Buy a food that passes the checks above and that your dog can actually eat. A healthy dog who is merely choosy is a different shopping job, covered in [dog food for picky eaters](/dog-food-for-picky-eaters/). The label checks on this page still come first. The AAHA [nutrition and weight management guidelines](https://www.aaha.org/resources/2021-aaha-nutrition-and-weight-management-guidelines/) treat a nutritional assessment as part of a regular visit: body condition, muscle, the actual diet, and the calories. A perfect label fed at the wrong amount is still the wrong plan.

The names in the table are examples of mainstream complete foods: Purina Pro Plan, Hill's Science Diet, Royal Canin size-specific lines, and Iams. They are listed because they are the sort of companies that publish adequacy statements and employ nutrition staff, which is the bar this page is willing to describe. They are not listed because this site ran a feeding trial, a palatability panel, or a stool-score study. Andrew Virts still has to open the current bags and confirm the listings. Match the life stage. An adult maintenance food is the usual target for a healthy grown dog. A dog with a diagnosed disease may need a veterinary diet instead, and that decision is the clinic's. If you are switching because of diarrhea, do the short bland-diet plan in the [diarrhea guide](/dog-food-for-diarrhea/) before you commit to a new everyday bag.

## How to switch without creating a new problem

Even a better food can cause soft stool if you dump it in the bowl all at once. Mix a quarter of the new food into the old for a couple of days, then half, then mostly the new food, across about a week. Go slower for a dog with a touchy gut. Keep treats steady so you can see what the dinner change did.

Write down the exact formula name. "The blue bag" is not a recall check. Photograph the adequacy statement and the lot code when you open a new sack. If the dog loses weight, gains weight, or starts refusing the food, the next stop is the clinic, not a second unplanned switch. Appetite problems have their own page: [dog not eating food](/dog-not-eating/).

## What a careful owner can ignore

Ignore ranking badges the brand gave itself. Ignore "vet recommended" with no description of who was asked. Ignore ingredient lists that lead with a fresh meat and then hide the rest of the protein in fractions you cannot add up. Ignore anyone who tells you a single brand is poison based on a screenshot, unless that screenshot is a current recall of the lot in your bin.

The AVMA note on [newer AAFCO labeling](https://www.avma.org/news/aafco-adopts-consumer-friendly-pet-food-labeling-guidelines) is a reminder that labels are getting clearer on purpose. Use that clarity. The brand to avoid is the one that still will not say, in the legally meaningful sentence, that the food is complete for the dog you have.

## How to read the adequacy sentence

The sentence is usually on the back or side, in small type, and it is more important than the front panel. You are looking for the species, the life stage, and the basis of the claim. A food "formulated to meet the nutritional levels established by the AAFCO Dog Food Nutrient Profiles for maintenance" is an adult maintenance food. A food that also says "including growth of large size dogs" is making a puppy claim a small-breed adult food is not making. If the sentence is missing, or it only says the food is "intended for intermittent or supplemental feeding," it is not a sole diet.

Photograph that sentence when you open the bag. If a company redesigns the label and the sentence gets vaguer, that is a reason to pause, not a reason to trust the new font. Ask who formulated the recipe and whether they can provide a typical analysis. You do not need to become a nutritionist to notice a company that answers with a slogan. Write the answer down. If the dog later gets sick, the clinic will ask what the food claimed to be, not what the advertisement felt like.

Price is not on this checklist. An expensive incomplete food is still incomplete. A modest complete food that the dog eats, at the right number of calories, beats a rotating set of boutique bags that never stay in the house long enough to judge. The AAHA guidelines are about the dog's body condition over time. Use that as the scoreboard, not a star rating this site refuses to invent.
