A dog not eating food is a symptom with a long menu of causes, and the internet usually offers the least useful one first: a more exciting dinner. Sometimes the dog is nauseated, in pain, or done with a bowl that is too big. Sometimes the house is loud. Sometimes the dog is sick enough that a new topper wastes the hour you should have spent calling a clinic.

This guide splits the problem into dogs you can try a few calm things with, and dogs you should not experiment on. "Dog not eating" and "dog not eating food" are the same search for most owners. The answer depends on who the dog is and what else is happening.

## Call first, before you shop

Contact a veterinarian the same day if any of these are true:

- The dog is a puppy or a toy breed. Small bodies run out of energy quickly, and waiting overnight is a real risk.
- There is vomiting, repeated drooling, or a hard, painful, or suddenly large belly.
- There is diarrhea as well, especially with blood, or the dog will not drink. Use [what to feed a dog with diarrhea](/dog-food-for-diarrhea/) only after you know the dog is stable enough for home care.
- The dog is weak, hiding, crying, or panting at rest.
- The dog might have eaten something toxic, a bone, a toy, or a lot of fat.
- The appetite has been fading for more than a day in an adult, or the dog is losing weight.

The Merck manual's [introduction to digestive disorders of dogs](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/digestive-disorders-of-dogs/introduction-to-digestive-disorders-of-dogs) is a sober list of how the gut announces trouble. Loss of appetite is on that list because it is common and because it is nonspecific. You will not sort nausea from dental pain from a foreign body by reading the ingredient panel of a new bag.

The AVMA's [pet owner care pages](https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/petcare) are the right tone for this decision: when you are unsure, ask someone who can examine the animal. This site cannot.

## A skipped meal in a bright adult

If the dog is an adult, still playing or at least comfortable, still drinking, and free of the signs above, you can watch one missed meal. Leave water down. Pick the bowl up after twenty minutes so the food does not become a warm petri dish. Offer the next meal at the normal time.

Do not stand over the bowl narrating. Do not start a buffet. A dog who learns that refusal produces chicken, cheese, and a hand-fed meatball will refuse the ordinary food tomorrow. The ASPCA's [dog nutrition tips](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/dog-nutrition-tips) are aimed at everyday feeding, and the useful piece here is consistency.

Things that are reasonable for that one bright dog:

- Serve the usual food in a quiet room, away from a visitor, a new puppy, or a child who hovers.
- Warm a wet food slightly so it smells stronger. Test it on your wrist. Hot food burns mouths.
- Offer the meal from a clean bowl. A sour bowl is a reason to walk away.
- Skip the high-value chew for that day so hunger has a chance to matter.
- Measure what you feed. Many "picky" dogs are full because the cup is a large cup and the treats are invisible calories. The AAHA [nutrition guidelines](https://www.aaha.org/resources/2021-aaha-nutrition-and-weight-management-guidelines/) put body condition and the actual diet on the exam, which is the grown-up version of this step.

Things that are a poor idea:

- Onion or garlic broth. Both are on the ASPCA [people-foods-to-avoid](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/people-foods-avoid-feeding-your-pets) list.
- A stack of new proteins in one day. You will not know what caused the next case of diarrhea.
- Withholding water to "make them hungry."
- Forcing food into the mouth of a nauseated dog.

## Dental pain, nausea, and stress

A dog who approaches the bowl, sniffs, and backs away may be nauseated or may hurt when chewing. Dropped food, chewing on one side, bad breath, and pawing at the face are reasons to book a dental exam rather than buy a softer marketing diet. A soft food can be a bridge if the clinic agrees. It is not a diagnosis.

Nausea is the other common pattern. Grass eating, lip licking, and a dog who wants the walk but not the dinner fit it. Those dogs need an exam if it lasts, because the causes run from a dietary indiscretion to disease you cannot see. Do not stack antacids from the human cabinet onto the problem. Doses and drugs are not interchangeable.

Stress is real and easy to over-call. A boarding stay, fireworks, or a moved crate can suppress appetite for a meal. A week of not eating is not "just stress" until a veterinarian has said the dog is well. Pain hides. So does kidney disease, liver disease, and a belly full of something that does not belong there.

## When the food itself is the suspect

Switching foods can cause a dog to refuse the new bag, or to eat it and then feel awful. If you changed brands this week, you can go back to the previous food, as long as that food was complete and the dog was well on it. Read [dog food brands to avoid](/dog-food-brands-to-avoid/) before the next switch so the replacement at least has an adequacy statement and a company you can call.

A food that is stale, rancid, or bug-ridden is a simple fix: throw it out. Fat that has oxidized smells wrong to a dog even when it still smells faint to you. Store the bag closed, and do not keep an open sack in a hot garage.

Prescription diets are a special case. If the dog is on a urinary, kidney, or hypoallergenic food and stops eating, call the clinic that prescribed it. Replacing it with a tasty grocery food can undo the treatment. A veterinary recovery diet such as a convalescent canned food is sometimes used in hospital. It is not the right secret weapon for a healthy dog who is lobbying for chicken. The card on this page says TODO for that reason.

## Toppers, and how they go wrong

A teaspoon of plain cooked chicken or a splash of warm water on the usual food is a small experiment. A permanent half-cup of mix-ins is a different diet, and it is often a less balanced one. The WSAVA [nutrition toolkit](https://wsava.org/global-guidelines/global-nutrition-guidelines/) is written so clinics ask what the dog really eats, including the extras. You should be able to answer that question in one sentence.

If a topper is the only way the dog will eat, tell the veterinarian. There may be a complete wet food that removes the need for the topper, or there may be a medical reason the dog will not eat the therapeutic diet. Guessing with a rotation of five proteins is how food trials get ruined. If you are in the middle of an elimination diet, add nothing. Call the clinic that set it up.

## Puppies and seniors

Puppies eat like it is their job, until they don't. A puppy who skips a meal and seems quiet should be seen. Do not apply the "adults can skip a meal" rule to a ten-week-old dog. The same is true for many toy breeds at any age.

Seniors can have a smaller appetite and still be healthy, and they can have a smaller appetite because something hurts. Weight loss is the line. Weigh the dog, or have the clinic weigh the dog, rather than arguing from memory about the ribs. Dental disease, arthritis that makes standing at the bowl hard, and chronic illness are all common. A raised bowl is a comfort for some dogs and a choking risk if it is the wrong setup. Ask before you renovate the kitchen.

## What to write down before the visit

Clinics get a better answer when you can say:

- When the dog last ate a full meal
- Whether water intake went up, down, or stayed the same
- Any vomiting, diarrhea, or straining
- Every food, treat, and medication in the last two days, including the stolen ones
- Whether this has happened before, and what the last diagnosis was

That list beats a photo of six candidate bags. Buy a new food after someone has decided a new food is the right tool. The names lower on this page are not that decision. ## Measure the food before you call the dog picky

Use a kitchen scale if you have one, or at least the same cup every day, and write the amount next to the calorie number on the bag. Two "cups" from a coffee mug can be a full extra meal. A dog who walks away from a bowl that already covers the day's calories is not a mystery. Treats, dental chews, and the other dog's leftovers count. Subtract them before you decide the dinner is being rejected.

Offer the measured meal for fifteen or twenty minutes and then pick it up. Free feeding a reluctant dog turns the bowl into furniture. A second chance at the next scheduled meal is fair. A handful of cheese between those meals is how the schedule collapses. If the dog eats eagerly the moment you add a topper and ignores the balanced food, tell the clinic the size of the topper. They would rather adjust a complete food than bless a growing pile of chicken.

Water still stays down the whole time. A dog who stops drinking is not in the "wait for the next meal" group, even if the rest of the day looked ordinary. That phone call is the whole point of drawing a line between a skipped dinner and a sick dog.
